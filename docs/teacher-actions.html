DLO 1 Get the student's attention before speaking

Get the student's attention before speaking

Make sure that the student knows that you are about to communicate. Use an appropriate visual signal, their name, or another agreed method of gaining attention. Avoid beginning important instructions while facing away from the class (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

Why does this matter?

Students who are deaf or hard of hearing may rely on visual information to supplement or access spoken communication. Gaining the student's attention before speaking provides an opportunity for the student to orient towards the teacher and access available visual information (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.; Marschark et al., 2011).

This is particularly important when the information being communicated is essential for learning, such as instructions, explanations, changes to an activity or safety information. If communication begins before the student is attending, part of the message may be missed before the student has had an opportunity to access the information.

Gaining attention should also be understood as an individualised practice. Students who are deaf or hard of hearing have diverse communication preferences and may use different combinations of spoken language, sign language, hearing technology and visual communication strategies (Marschark et al., 2011). Teachers should therefore work with the student and relevant support professionals to establish an appropriate method of gaining attention.

Classroom scenario

Maya is working quietly while the class completes an activity. You are about to give an important instruction that everyone needs to hear.

What should you do first?

Key teaching practice

Establish with the student how they prefer to have their attention gained and use this consistently before communicating important information (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

  • Use the student's name where appropriate.
  • Use an agreed visual signal or gesture.
  • Wait until the student is attending before beginning important communication.
  • Avoid calling out repeatedly from across the classroom.
  • Avoid beginning instructions while facing away from the class.
Reflect

Think about your own classroom practice. Do you ever begin speaking while students are still looking at their books, working on the board, moving around the room or talking with another student?

What simple change could you make to ensure that students know when important communication is about to begin?

DLO 2 Face the student when speaking

Face the student when speaking

Where a student's communication relies on visual information, seeing the teacher's face can be important. Teachers should avoid speaking while writing on the board, walking around the room, or covering their mouth, as these actions can reduce students' visual access to communication (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

Why does this matter?

For some students who are deaf or hard of hearing, visual information can support access to spoken communication. Facing the student provides access to facial expression, mouth movements and other visual communication cues that may contribute to understanding (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.; Marschark et al., 2011).

In contrast, speaking while facing the board, moving around the classroom or covering the mouth can make it more difficult for a student to maintain visual access to the teacher. These barriers can be particularly significant when the teacher is providing new information, explaining instructions or discussing important concepts.

This does not mean that teachers must remain completely still or face the student at all times. Rather, teachers should be conscious of when visual access is particularly important and position themselves so that students can clearly see them when communicating essential information. This is part of creating an accessible classroom environment without reducing the expectations placed on students.

Classroom scenario

You are explaining an important task to the class. Some students rely on visual information to support their understanding.

You need to continue writing instructions on the board while explaining the task.

What is the most accessible approach?

Key teaching practice

Position yourself so that students who rely on visual information can clearly see your face when you are communicating important information (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

  • Face the class when giving important instructions.
  • Stop writing on the board when speaking about important information.
  • Avoid covering your mouth when communicating.
  • Avoid speaking while walking away from students.
  • Consider your position, lighting and visibility when communicating.
  • Allow students to indicate if they need you to reposition yourself or repeat information.

Beyond "facing the student"

Facing the student is one part of a broader approach to accessible classroom communication. Teachers also need to consider lighting, classroom position, background distractions, the distance between teacher and student, and whether other people are speaking at the same time (Mealings et al., 2025).

These factors can interact with one another. For example, a student may be able to see the teacher clearly but still experience difficulty accessing communication if the teacher is moving frequently, the room is poorly lit, or several students are speaking simultaneously. Effective practice therefore involves considering the whole communication environment rather than relying on a single adjustment.

Most importantly, teachers should recognise that students who are deaf or hard of hearing have different communication preferences and needs. The most appropriate approach should be discussed with the individual student and, where appropriate, their family and relevant support professionals (Marschark et al., 2011).

Reflect

Think about where you normally stand when giving instructions. Are you facing the class, writing on the board, moving around the room, or standing in a position where some students may have difficulty seeing you?

What could you change about your position or movement to improve students' visual access to your communication?

DLO 3 Make instructions visible

Make instructions visible

Provide important instructions in a visual form as well as verbally. Written instructions, diagrams, key vocabulary, worked examples and lesson outlines can reduce the need for students to rely on hearing information alone (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

Providing information through multiple modes can also support students to revisit information at their own pace. Rather than requiring students to remember a sequence of instructions that was communicated verbally, visual information can remain available throughout the learning activity (Department for Education, Government of South Australia, n.d.).

Why does this matter?

Classroom learning frequently depends on spoken instructions. Teachers may explain what students need to do, introduce new vocabulary, describe a process or provide additional information while students are expected to begin working. For students who are deaf or hard of hearing, relying on spoken information alone can create a barrier to accessing this information.

Providing an equivalent visual form gives students another pathway to access important information. This might include written instructions, diagrams, images, key vocabulary, demonstrations, worked examples, lesson outlines or visual sequences (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

Visual supports are also useful because they remain available after the teacher has finished speaking. Students can return to the instructions while completing the task rather than needing to rely entirely on memory or ask for the information to be repeated.

This approach is consistent with inclusive teaching principles because the teacher is not lowering the learning expectations. Instead, the teacher is providing different ways for students to access the same learning intention and classroom activity (Department of Education, 2026).

What can "making instructions visible" look like?
  • Written instructions: Provide a short, numbered sequence of what students need to do.
  • Diagrams and images: Represent processes, relationships or concepts visually.
  • Key vocabulary: Display important subject-specific words and their meanings.
  • Worked examples: Show students what a completed task or process looks like.
  • Lesson outlines: Make the structure and sequence of the lesson visible.
  • Demonstrations: Show a process or skill rather than relying entirely on verbal explanation.

Visual information supports independence

A key benefit of keeping instructions visible is that it can support student independence. When instructions remain accessible, students can check what they need to do without necessarily waiting for the teacher to repeat the information.

This can be particularly useful during practical, collaborative or multi-step activities where students need to move between different stages of a task. Visual supports can provide a consistent reference point throughout the lesson rather than requiring students to retain a lengthy verbal explanation.

Visual supports should therefore be considered more than an accommodation added specifically for one student. They can provide useful access to information for the whole class while particularly reducing barriers for students who have difficulty accessing spoken information (Department of Education, 2026).

Try this in your classroom

When giving important instructions, ask yourself: "Could a student understand what to do if they missed part of what I said?"

If not, add a visual support such as a short written sequence, diagram, key words, example or lesson outline.

The aim is not to replace spoken communication, but to provide students with another reliable way to access and revisit important information.

DLO 4 Consider the classroom environment

Consider the classroom environment

The classroom environment is one driving force behind the challenges faced by students who are deaf or hard of hearing. Hear For You (2020) explore this notion, stating that classroom arrangement (seating arrangement, lighting, and overall classroom organisation) directly influences the learning experiences and capabilities of deaf and hard of hearing students.

Mealings et al. (2025) further explores how teachers' arrangement of their classroom environment can result in students who are deaf or hard of hearing experiencing challenges through greater background noise, competing speakers, poor acoustics, teacher distance and movement, missing spoken information, listening fatigue, and reduced support, confidence and social inclusion.

Background noise and competing speakers

Classroom noise makes it very difficult for students who are deaf or hard of hearing to hear, understand and engage with spoken information from the lesson. Mealings et al. (2025) outline how this becomes especially problematic during group and guided learning activities as multiple students and groups may be talking at once.

Poor acoustics and teacher distance/movement

Spoken information is further impacted by echoes, reverberations, and teacher movement around the room. These factors all affect clarity of teacher speech. Mealings et al. (2025) builds on this, emphasising how clarity of speech and teacher distance and movement were among the strongest environmental factors challenging students who are deaf or hard of hearing ability to listen, understand and engage with classroom learning.

Why the environment matters

Environmental factors can increase the effort required to listen and understand classroom communication. Reducing unnecessary noise, improving positioning and considering acoustics can therefore support access to learning.

Consider whether the student has a clear view of the teacher and other speakers, whether background noise can be reduced, and whether the classroom arrangement supports comfortable listening.

DLO 5 Manage classroom discussion

Manage classroom discussion

Make it clear who is speaking during class discussion. Encourage students to speak one at a time and repeat or summarise important contributions when necessary. This can be particularly important when students are working in groups or when several people are speaking from different parts of the room (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

Why does this matter?

Classroom discussion can create additional challenges for students who are deaf or hard of hearing because communication may involve multiple speakers, rapid changes between speakers and information coming from different locations around the classroom. These factors can make it difficult to identify who is speaking and follow the flow of the discussion (Mealings et al., 2025).

The challenge can become greater during group work or whole-class discussion when students speak over one another. Background noise and competing speakers can increase the effort required to listen and understand spoken information, potentially affecting participation and access to learning (Mealings et al., 2025).

Teachers can reduce these barriers by establishing clear turn-taking, identifying speakers and repeating or summarising important contributions. These practices make the structure of classroom communication more predictable and provide students with additional opportunities to access important information (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

Classroom scenario

You are facilitating a whole-class discussion. Several students begin responding at the same time, while other students are talking in small groups around the room.

Which approach would make the discussion more accessible?

Key teaching practice

Structure classroom discussion so that students can identify who is speaking and follow important contributions. This is particularly important when several students are participating (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.; Mealings et al., 2025).

  • Establish clear turn-taking.
  • Encourage students to speak one at a time.
  • Identify or acknowledge who is speaking where necessary.
  • Repeat or summarise important student contributions.
  • Reduce unnecessary background conversations during important discussion.
  • Ensure students can see the person speaking where visual access is important.
  • Check that important information has been communicated clearly before moving on.

Group work and multiple speakers

Group work can be particularly challenging because students may speak from different positions, turn towards one another, speak simultaneously or move around the room. Mealings et al. (2025) identifies competing speakers, background noise and teacher distance and movement as environmental factors that can affect the listening experiences of students who are deaf or hard of hearing.

Teachers can make group activities more accessible by explicitly teaching students how to take turns, reducing unnecessary background noise and establishing routines for identifying the current speaker. These strategies can benefit the whole class while reducing barriers for students who have difficulty accessing multiple speakers.

Importantly, the goal is not to prevent natural classroom interaction. Rather, teachers can structure interaction so that students have equitable opportunities to contribute and access the ideas of their peers.

Repeat and summarise

When a student makes an important contribution, consider briefly repeating or summarising the key idea before moving to the next speaker. This can help ensure that important information is clearly available to the whole class and can provide another opportunity to access the contribution (Northern Ireland Department of Education, n.d.).

This practice can also strengthen learning for all students by highlighting the key idea and connecting individual contributions to the broader lesson.

Reflect

Think about the last whole-class discussion or group activity you facilitated.

DLO 6 Check understanding without singling the student out

Check understanding without singling the student out

Build regular opportunities for all students to check and demonstrate their understanding. Avoid assuming that a student has understood simply because they appear to be listening. Encourage questions and provide alternative ways to revisit information when needed.

Use strategies such as visual prompts, written instructions, demonstrations, examples and opportunities for students to ask questions or revisit information.

DLO 7 Incorporate sign languages into classrooms

Incorporating Sign Languages into Classrooms

Sign language can be an important part of language, identity and education for some students who are deaf or hard of hearing. Communication preferences vary considerably between students, so teachers should avoid assuming that every Deaf student uses Auslan. Instead, schools should work with students, families and relevant Deaf education professionals to understand the student's preferred language and communication methods.

Auslan K–10 Syllabus (2023)

NSW introduced the Auslan K–10 Syllabus in 2023. Implementation commenced in 2026, giving NSW schools the opportunity to offer Auslan within K–6 language programs, as the selected language for the mandatory 100 hours of language study in Years 7–10, and as a Stage 5 language elective.

The syllabus recognises Auslan as a distinct language with its own grammar and vocabulary and emphasises the relationship between language, culture and identity. It also highlights the importance of consultation with the Deaf community when Auslan is taught in schools.

Indigenous Sign Languages

Australian Indigenous sign languages are an important part of the diverse linguistic landscape of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. They are not simply alternative communication systems for Deaf people. In some communities, Indigenous sign languages may function alongside spoken languages and be used by both hearing and non-hearing community members.

This is particularly important for teachers working in communities where Indigenous sign languages are used. For some students, an Indigenous sign language may be part of the language used in their home or community. Teachers therefore need to consider the student's broader linguistic and cultural context rather than assuming that Auslan or spoken English is their primary means of communication.

Why this matters for teachers

Understanding the linguistic diversity of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities can help teachers avoid making assumptions about how students communicate. Language, culture, identity and community are closely connected, and these factors should be considered when planning inclusive teaching and learning.

DLO 8 Utilise student strengths

By removing challenges for students who are deaf or hard of hearing, teachers are able to create a learning environment that is inclusive, engaging and supportive. The DOE (2026) outlines that to create this type of inclusive environment for all students, teachers must also recognise and draw upon students' strengths, needs, interests and prior learning.

Marschark et al. (2011) explores the strengths of deaf and hard of hearing students, highlighting skills such as visual-spatial processing and learning, mental imagery abilities, and visual-spatial memory. The Department for Education, Government of South Australia (n.d.) further outlines how students who are deaf or hard of hearing often demonstrate the strength of learning through hands-on, kinaesthetic activities.

Four strengths teachers can utilise

1. Visual-spatial processing

The Department for Education, Government of South Australia (n.d.) outlines that students who are deaf or hard of hearing often learn effectively by processing information visually and spatially. Teachers can focus on this strength, supporting students' visual-spatial processing and learning through visual representations, spatial organisation and visually presented information (Marschark et al., 2011).

Rather than relying heavily on verbal explanations, teachers can incorporate diagrams, graphics, maps, images and spatial arrangements, building on students' strengths to support, include and engage them in positive learning.

Teacher practice: Use diagrams, maps, images, models and spatial organisation to represent information visually.

2. Mental imagery abilities

Students who are deaf or hard of hearing often possess strengths in visual learning and mental imagery (Department for Education, Government of South Australia, n.d.). Teachers can build on these skills by incorporating and designing instruction that allows students to utilise their mental imagery abilities.

For example, students could visualise how something changes or moves, rotate shapes mentally, or imagine a setting in a story. Marschark et al. (2011) further highlights that students who are deaf or hard of hearing often equal or exceed hearing individuals in generating and manipulating mental images.

Teacher practice: Ask students to visualise movement, change, shapes, objects, locations or settings as part of learning activities.

3. Visual-spatial memory

Marschark et al. (2011) outlines how students who are deaf or hard of hearing, in particular native signers, often demonstrate stronger memory through visual-spatial relationships.

Students may retain information more easily through visual location, spatial relationships, patterns and organisation rather than long verbal explanations of information. Teachers can support, engage and include students who are deaf or hard of hearing by presenting information visually and spatially, whilst also explicitly teaching various strategies to remember different types of information (Marschark et al., 2011).

Teacher practice: Organise information through visual sequences, patterns, locations, diagrams and spatial relationships to support memory.

4. Hands-on, kinaesthetic learning

Students who are deaf or hard of hearing may possess strong hands-on, kinaesthetic learning skills (Department for Education, Government of South Australia, n.d.).

By incorporating hands-on activities into learning, teachers can include, engage and support students who are deaf or hard of hearing by building on their strengths, needs and interests.

Teacher practice: Provide opportunities to manipulate objects, create, experiment, move, build, perform and learn through direct experience.

Match teaching to strengths

Marschark et al. (2011) emphasises how deaf and hard of hearing students have the capacity to make equivalent learning achievements to their peers when teaching is matched to their strengths, interests and needs.

The goal is not to assume that all students who are deaf or hard of hearing learn in the same way. Instead, teachers should identify individual strengths and provide opportunities for students to use those strengths as part of their learning.

Quick reflection: Think about a lesson you teach. Where could you replace a mainly verbal explanation with a visual, spatial, mental imagery or hands-on approach?
