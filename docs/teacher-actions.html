Teacher Actions

Creating an Inclusive Classroom

Teachers play an important role in ensuring that students who are deaf or hard of hearing can participate fully in classroom learning. Effective support is not simply about providing technology or additional assistance. It also involves understanding how students communicate, anticipating barriers to participation, and adapting everyday teaching practices.

“Deaf pupils can learn as much as their hearing peers when they are taught by teachers who recognise and can adapt teaching methods and materials to accommodate their strengths and needs.”

Northern Ireland Department of Education, Deaf Friendly Schools Guidance

A useful starting point

Do not assume what a student can or cannot hear, understand or access. Ask the student, their family and relevant support professionals about their communication preferences and the strategies that work best for them.

Everyday Teacher Actions

Many adjustments that support students who are deaf or hard of hearing are simple changes to everyday classroom practice. The aim is to reduce unnecessary barriers while maintaining high expectations for learning.

Get the student's attention before speaking

Make sure the student knows that you are about to communicate. Use an appropriate visual signal, their name, or another agreed method of gaining attention. Avoid beginning important instructions while facing away from the class.

Face the student when speaking

Where the student's communication relies on visual information, seeing the teacher's face can be important. Avoid speaking while writing on the board, walking around the room, or covering your mouth.

Make instructions visible

Provide important instructions in a visual form as well as verbally. Written instructions, diagrams, key vocabulary, worked examples and lesson outlines can reduce the need for students to rely on hearing information alone.

Manage classroom discussion

Make it clear who is speaking during class discussion. Encourage students to speak one at a time and repeat or summarise important contributions when necessary. This can be particularly important when students are working in groups or when several people are speaking from different parts of the room.

Consider the classroom environment

Think about where the student is seated, the position of the teacher, lighting, background noise and the location of other speakers. Small environmental changes can make it easier for students to access communication and reduce listening effort.

Check understanding without singling the student out

Build regular opportunities for all students to check and demonstrate their understanding. Avoid assuming that a student has understood simply because they appear to be listening. Encourage questions and provide alternative ways to revisit information when needed.

Incorporating Sign Languages into Classrooms

Sign language can be an important part of language, identity and education for some students who are deaf or hard of hearing. Communication preferences vary considerably between students, so teachers should avoid assuming that every Deaf student uses Auslan. Instead, schools should work with students, families and relevant Deaf education professionals to understand the student's preferred language and communication methods.

Auslan K–10 Syllabus (2023)

NSW introduced the Auslan K–10 Syllabus in 2023. Implementation commenced in 2026, giving NSW schools the opportunity to offer Auslan within K–6 language programs, as the selected language for the mandatory 100 hours of language study in Years 7–10, and as a Stage 5 language elective.

The syllabus recognises Auslan as a distinct language with its own grammar and vocabulary and emphasises the relationship between language, culture and identity. It also highlights the importance of consultation with the Deaf community when Auslan is taught in schools.

Indigenous Sign Languages

Australian Indigenous sign languages are an important part of the diverse linguistic landscape of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. They are not simply alternative communication systems for Deaf people. In some communities, Indigenous sign languages may function alongside spoken languages and be used by both hearing and non-hearing community members.

This is particularly important for teachers working in communities where Indigenous sign languages are used. For some students, an Indigenous sign language may be part of the language used in their home or community. Teachers therefore need to consider the student's broader linguistic and cultural context rather than assuming that Auslan or spoken English is their primary means of communication.

Why this matters for teachers

Understanding the linguistic diversity of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities can help teachers avoid making assumptions about how students communicate. Language, culture, identity and community are closely connected, and these factors should be considered when planning inclusive teaching and learning.

Work With the Student, Not Just the Support

Perhaps the most important teacher action is to involve the student in decisions about their learning. A strategy that works well for one student may not work for another. Students who are deaf or hard of hearing are not a homogeneous group, and their communication, educational and social needs can change across different contexts.

Teachers should therefore work collaboratively with students, families, interpreters, support teachers and other relevant professionals. The goal is not to lower expectations or remove students from ordinary classroom experiences, but to remove barriers that prevent them from demonstrating what they know and can do.

Reflection

Think about a class you currently teach, or have taught recently. What information in that class is primarily communicated through listening?

What could you change so that the same information is also accessible visually or through another form of communication?
